- Crime is typically defined as any action which breaks a law 
  and results in a punishment, for example imprisonment. The
  problem with defining crime in this way is that laws can 
  change from time to time and place to place, and not all 
  acts that break the law are punished.

- Cultural issues: Actions considered a crime in one culture 
  are sometimes not classed as a crime in another culture. 
  For example, the practice of having more than one wife is 
  a crime in the UK (bigamy), but in some cultures, this would 
  not be seen as a crime (polygamy).

- Historical issues: Definitions of crime change over time, 
  for example homosexuality was a crime until 1967 in the UK 
  and remains a crime in many Asian and African countries.

## Measuring crime: Official statistics
- These are government records of numbers reported to the
  police and recorded in official figures. They are published
  annually by the home office. They are used by the government
  as information which can help to develop crime prevention
  strategies.
- Evaluation:
  - Official statistics underestimate the extent of crime. 
    They only include reported crimes, which may be only 
    around 25% of the total crimes committed.

## Victim surveys
- These ask victims about their experience of crime. For 
  example, in the UK 50,000 randomly selected households take
  part in the 'Crime Survey for England and Wales' each 
  years. The office for national statistics uses the results
  of the survey to detect trends and patterns.

- They can be more accurate than official statistics, as they
  include crimes not reported to the police. Therefore, the
  information gained may be more trustworthy.
- They rely on memory and self-report, which can be unreliable.
  Participants may not remember when the crime took place - 
  it may be longed than a year ago, for instance. This can 
  lead to misleading information.

## Offender surveys
- These records examples of crimes through asking offenders
  to give details about crimes they have committed. Groups
  of likely offenders are often used, based on risk factors
  such as background, past offences and so on. An example
  was the 'Offender Crime and Justice Survery (2003-2006)'.

# Offender Profiling
- Offender proffiling aims to creating a list of likely 
  suspects for a crime, by narrowing down the possible field
  of perpetrators. This is done by proffesional profilers
  and the police, who use evidence and the crime scene to 
  generate hypotheses about the characteristics of the killer,
  such as their age, occupation, and so on.

- There are several ways to do this:
  - Top down approach,
  - Bottom up approach.

## Top-Down Approach
- This originated in the US (particularly, the FBI) in the
  1970s. The characteristics of the crime, and the likely 
  offender, is matched to a pre-existing 'template', 
  provided by the FBI.
- It is deductive and applies ideas, theories and concepts
  to information at the crime scene basing its conclusions
  on this - it tests existing theories and ideas.
- For instance, a murder could be classified as 'organised'
  or 'disorganised'. This is then used to guide police 
  investigations. The template suggest that the criminal 
  has a particular way of working ('modus operandi' or 'MO'),
  which correlates with an aspect of their personality,
  occupation, or something else about them.

- Templates were created in-depth interviews with 36 serial
  killers such as Ted Bundy and Charles Manson.
- The interview covered details such as:
  - was each murder planned and if so, how?
  - how was each murder carried out?
  - what went through the mind of the killer when they were
    commiting the murder?
  - whether each murderer had regular fantasies about killing?
  - what each murderer did in between killings?

- The data obtained from these interviews, combined with the 
  crimr scense helped Ressler and his collegues to identify
  two typologies of murderer.

## Organised or disorganised types
Organised offenders:
- They show evidence of advanced planning, and will 'choose'
  particular victims. They worl in an emotionally detached
  way, leave little evidence, and tends to be intelligent,
  in skilled professional occupations. They are also able
  to display normal social and sexual behaviour.

Disorganised offenders:
- They tend to be the opposite, in the sense that the crime 
  (such as a murder) is not pre-planned, but is done on the 
  spur of the moment, perhaps in response to a particular
  emotional trigger. The body will ofen therefore not be 
  disposed of, because no plans have been made to do so, and
  more evidence is left behind. Such offenders tend to have
  lower IQs, be in unskilled work (or unemployed), and have
  histories of psychological and/or sexual dysfunction, in 
  addition to living alone.

## Constructing and FBI Profile
- There are 4 stages to the process:
1. Data assimilation: reviewing the available evidence
2. Crime scene classification: organised or disorganised
3. Crime scene reconstruction: a hypothesis of what happened
   in light of the evidence gathered
4. Profile generation: a hypothesis of the characteristics of
   the likely offender-background, personality traits, 
   occupation, and so on

## Research into the top-down approach
- Snook et al. (2007) conducted a meta-analysis of profiling
  research and concluded that most profiling was based on 
  little more than common-sense justifications and that 
  profilers were not significantly better at predicting 
  offense behaviours than non-profilers but were slightly
  better at identifying overall offender characteristics

- Canter et al. (2004) - 100 American serial killers were
  analysed: the results showed that the label organised/
  disorganised could not easily be applied to the crimes
  and that there may be a subset of organised characteristics
  that typify serial murder, whereas disorganised murder 
  rarely fits into a distinct typology

## Evaluation of top-down approach
- Top-down approaches work best for particular types of 
  crime such as premeditated murder, rape, torture and so on.
  They work much less well for crimes such as burglary, 
  because the crime scene reveals little about the offender.
  Therefore, the top-down approach can only be properly used
  for certain crimes.

- The top-down approach relies on the prediction that 
  personality characteristics remain fairly stable over time,
  sometimes over years or decades. Therefore, it does not 
  consider that personality may be strongly affected by 
  external factors, reducing the validity of this approach.

- The top-down approach may be too simplistic in its 
  categories. For instance, it would be hard for it to 
  explain a spontaneous killing carried out by an educated,
  proffesional person. It has been suggested that there may
  be overlap between the 'organised' and 'disorganised'
  categories, reducing the validity of the approach.

## Botton-up approach
- This was developed in the UK by David Canter (amongst 
  others). The aim is to generate a profile of the offender
  by looking at the available evidence.
- There are no fixed 'typologies' that will be attempted to
  be matched to the offender. Instead, a profile should
  amerge soley from the evidence of each case.

Investigative psychology:
- This is an attempt to use statistical procedures and 
  psychological theory. Statistics are used to create a 
  prediction of behaviour that is likely to occur in crimes. 
  Specific details of an offense are matched to this to 
  create details about the offender.
- The idea of 'interpersonal coherance' is key - how the 
  offender acts during the crime is likely to relate to 
  their actions in non-criminal situations.

- Geographic profiling: Rossmo (1997) proposed this method, 
  which involves looking at the location of crimes which 
  have been committed by the same offender ('crime mapping').
- Hypotheses can be generated about what the offender is 
  thinking 

# Biological explanations of offender behaviour

- One biological aproach is called the historical approach, developd by Lombroso (1876).
- Atavistic form:
  - Refers to features which belong to a more primitive era e.g. neaderthal.
- Lombroso argued that criminals were genetic/evolutionary throwbacks, meaning that they were more primitive than non-offenders.
  They would therefore best be seen as a 'sub-species' of humanity. These ideas helped to move explanations for criminal
  behaviour towards a more scientific realm.

- Atavistic characteristics:
  - As criminal behaviour is biologically determined, Lombroso suggested that there were also physical characteristics 
    accompanying the 'sub-species'. These characteristics could be used to identify criminals.
  - Lombroso even suggested that particular types of criminals had particular features. 

### Strengths
- Lombardo's theories were the first attempt to explain criminal behaviours in a more scientific way, by considering phisioligy
  and genetics. This helped pave the way for further investigation into evolutionary and biological explanations.
- Lombroso's work has been accused of racism, as many of the ativistic features are also features of people of African descent.
  The danger in accepting in accepting these theories would be that they could be used to support selective breeding (eugenics)
  in humans, which would be ethically unjustifiable today.
- DeLsi (2012) reviewed Lombroso's theory and concluded that while it has many - quite alarming - claims, it still stands as a 
  viable first attempt to a theory of criminality.

### Limitations
- Goring (1913) found that, when comparing 3000 criminals with 3000 non-criminals, there are no evidence of particular physical
  characteristics amongst criminals, weakening the suggestion that it is possible to identify potential offenders just by 
  sight.
- Lombroso's theory used no real methedology and was riven with bias.
- It could be argued that having facial features deemed 'agressive' may cause a self fulfilling prophecy.
- Suggests that criminals are born, not made, implying that criminal behaviour is innate and biologically fixed.
  - This is deterministic and denies the role of free will.
- Labelling individuals as criminals based on physicla appearance is highly socially sensetive and ethically problematic.

## Genetic explanations
- Genetic explanations suggest that particular genes, or combinations of them, are associated with criminal behaviour.
  - Lange (1930) looked at 13 identical (MZ) and 17 non-identical (DZ), where one twin in each pair had served time in prison.
    10 of the MZ twins, and only 2 of the DZ twins, had a co-twin also in prison, suggesting a genetic basis for criminal
    behaviour.
- Chistiansen (1977) found concordance rates of 33% of MZ and 12% for DZ twins for criminal behaviour, also showing a possible
  genetic link.

## Neural explanations
- Individuals diagnosed with ASPD may have a neural explanation for their condition. ASPD is characterised by lack of empathy
  and reduced emotional responses.

- Prefrontal cortex:
  - Raine has found through conducting brain imaging studies that individuals with antisocial personalities have reduced
    activity in their prefrontal cortex.

- Structural differences in the brain may also play a role in offending behaviour, particularly with regard to the limbic 
  system.

# Psychological explanations for offending behaviour

- Psychological explanations concentrate on factors such as 
  family and environmental influences on offending behaviour,
  cognitive factors and personality. It suggests that 
  behaviour is due to mental processes such as our 
  personality type (Eysenck), maladaptive thought patterns 
  (cognitive explanations), socially learnt behaviour
  (differential association theory) or unconscious processes
  psychodynamic.

- Eysneck (1947) proposed that behaviour could be measured 
  and represented along two dimensions - introversion/
  extraversion (E) and neuroticism/stability (N).

- Eysenck suggested that personality traits originate from 
  biology, therefore they are innate. He suggested this was 
  linked to the activity of the nervous system, which is 
  inherited. How easily the nervous system responds to 
  stimulus (arousal.
- Extraverts have na underactive nervous system, so 
  constantly seek excitement and interest, and don't learn 
  as well from mistakes.
- Introverts have an over-aroused nervous system, so 
  do not seek attention.

- Neurotic people are nervous and anxious and generally 
  unstable, are easy to upset and show obsessive behaviours 
  due to nervous system that is easily triggered by threats.
- Psychotism: measured on a scale of low to high, psychotics 
  are emotionally cold and do not feel compassion.

- The criminal personality: 
- Eysenck argued that the criminal personality is a 
  combination of extravert and neurotic characteristics, as
  well as psychotic characteristics.

- He claimed that people with the criminal personality are 
  more likely to engage in risk taking behaviours as a result
  of their high levels of extraversion; they are more likely 
  to be impulsive as a result of their neuroticism and they 
  are more likely to lack guilt and empathy as a result of 
  their psychotism.

- Socialisation: Eysenck saw criminal behaviour as 
  developmentally immature, as the criminal is concerned with
  instant gratification - instead of waiting before they can
  get something, they take it straight away.
- If an individual had a high N and E score, they would find 
  it hard to learn delayed gratification in childhood and be
  socialised properly. Therefore, they would be more likely to
  act in selfish, antisocial ways if given the chance.

- Measuring the criminal personality: Eysenck developed the 
  'Eysenck Personality inventory' (EPI), as test which 
  measures where respondents would be placed along the E and
  N dimensions.

## Evaluation
- Eysenck and Eysneck (1977) found that criminals scored 
  highly on E, N, P measures than a control group of 
  non-criminals, supporting the theory. However, Farrington 
  et al (1982) reviewed studies which showed that criminals 
  scored highly  on P measures, but not E and N. Therefore, 
  the evidence for the theory is mixed.

- McGurk and McDougall (1981) gave the EPI questionare to 100
  convicted inmates and 100 trade based students (e.g. 
  bricklaying), aged 17-20. Social class was controlled for.
  The results showed a higher number of people with extravert
  and psychotic personality types in the delinquent group.

- The EPI is a standardised measure which is easy to replicate
  in large numbers, which means that it has good reliablility.

- It has been suggested that there are many different types 
  of criminal personality, depending on what types of crimes 
  are involved and how they are carried out. Therefore, the 
  idea that there is one criminal personality 'type' has been 
  criticised as over-simplistic.
- Eysneck's theory depends on one stable criminal personality
  type over time. Moffit (1993) argues this is too simplistic 
  and suggests a dual taxonomy. Life course persistent, 
  people who are stable in offending behaviour over their 
  life and adolescent limited, those who stop anti-social 
  behaviour in adulthood. This better explains offending 
  figues that show 10x higher levels of crime in adolescence.

## Theory of moral reasoning
- Kohlberg's theory of moral developmen is a stage theory,
  as it sees the development of morality as linked to key
  developmental stages in a person's life.
- Criminals are more likely to be classed at a basic stage
  of moral development - the 'pre-conventional model'.
- Non-criminals tend to be at a higher stage - the 
  'conventional' or 'post-conventional' level.
- The pre-conventional level is characterised by a simple 
  need to avoid punishment and gain rewards, and as such is 
  more childlike level of reasoning. Crime may be commited
  if the criminal thinks they will get away with it, or if it
  brings a significant enough reward.
- It also means offenders are more like;y to be egocentric
  (self-centered) and less concerned with the rights of 
  others.
