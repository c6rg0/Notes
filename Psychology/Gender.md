# Sex and gender
- In psychology, sex is the classification of being male or
  female. 
- It is biologically determined - caused by 
  chromosomes and hormones.
- Therefore, different sexes will have different sex
  chrmosomes, genotypes (XX or XY), gene expression, hormone
  types and levels.
- It is fixed and cannot be changed, and is innate (due to 
  influence of nature only).

## Gender
- This is a social construt and allows individuals to identify
  themselves (self representation) as being either feminine 
  or masculine.
- It is influenced by psychological and social factors, the
  role of nature and nurture play a part, and is more fluid
  and open to change - this means it is more of a sliding
  scale, whereas sex tends to be a binary choice of male
  or female.
- This in turn influences behaviour, how people choose to 
  dress and also how they introduce themselves.
- Gender can be changed through gender reassignment surgery.

- Gender schema theory states that once children have 
  identified themselves as a girl or a boy, they are motivated
  to be like others in their gender group.
- This leads them to observe and imitate same-sex role models
  and conform to their sex-role expectations, which match 
  their gender schema.
- Sex and gender often align, but they do not cecessarily 
  have to.
- When there is a mismatch, it can cause distress and a
  feeling of being trapped in the 'wrong body', which is
  called gender dysphoria.

## Sex-role stereotypes
- Sex-role stereotypes are the result of social and cultural
  beliefs about what is normal for male and female behaviours.
- Societal expectations for boys and girls is different
  and children are taught these roles from a very early age.

## Sex and gender
- You cannot choose your sex, but you can choose your gender.
- With such differences between sex and gender comes 
  gender-identity disorder, which occurs when a person's
  sex is not in accordance with their gender.
- To resolve this, individuals may identify as being 
  transgender, and/or undergo gender transitioning. This 
  changes the person's sexual identity through changing 
  their reproductive organs and hormone levels.

- Many people view their sex and gender as being in accordance
  and this may have led to the development of sex-role
  stereotypes, which describes the expecations and 
  perceptions that individuals and society have as to what
  is appropriate behaviour for each sex.
- This leads to the development of stereotypes, as to how
  each gender should behave.
- Howver, this also results in 'justification' for
  discriminatory practices on the basis on sex, such as 
  denying women well-paid but potentially stressful work 
  opportunities in the fear that they will not be able to
  manage.

## Evaluation of sex and gender
- Strengths:
  - Sex-role stereotypes can differ substantially cross-
    culturally (Mead, 1935), suggesting that the 
    characteristics associated with sex-role stereotyping
    could be addressed by providing learning experiences for
    children that reinforce the idea of positive sex roles.
  - Research and day-to-day experience demonstrates that 
    sex-role stereotypes are reinforced by the media and so
    supports the theory that sex-role expectations are
    transmitted through sex-role stereotypes.

- Weaknesses:
  - Categorising behaviours, and qualities as either 
    masculine or feminine may restrict the positive roles
    that both males and females could play in society, like
    male child minders or female scientists.
  - However, it does not explain how individuals resist 
    being socialised into traditional sex roles.
  - Different stereotypes - there os unlikely to be a one-
    size-fits-all explanation of gender stereotypes, as 
    there are many different forms of stereotypes.

## Issues and debates
- The difference between sex and gender can be seen as an 
  example of the nature/nurture debate.
- While sex is determined from the moment of conception and
  at birth, gendder could be argued to be socially constructed
  through gender schemas composed of sex-role expectations.
- Biological psychologists argue that gender is innate,
  because, for example, women tend to be smaller and less 
  muscular than men, have higher-pictched voices etc.
- This sexual dimorphism is believed by psychologists of
  the biological approach to determine the psychological
  traits associated with each gender.

- Therefore, the biological approach argues that, in the
  same way that chromosomes and hormones determine an
  individual's sex, they also determine whether a person
  will behave in a more feminine or masculine way.
- However, if gender is determined by sex, this would mean
  that there would be none of the cross-cultural differences
  in masculine or feminine behaviour, identified by Mead 
  (1935) and others after her.
- Of course, nature and nurture interact and there is no 
  behaviour that can be exclusively one or the other, but sex
  owes more to nature and gender is created through sex-role
  stereotyping, which is more nature.

## Sex and temperament
- Margaret Mead (1935) conducted a classic study of cultural
  differences in Papua New Guinea.
- Her aim was to discover to what extent temperamental 
  differences between the sexes were culturally determined
  rather than innate.
- She examined 3 different tribes and found the:
  - Arapesh men and women to be gentle, responsive and 
    cooperative;
  - Mundugumur men and women were violent and aggressive,
    seeking power and position;
  - Tchambuli showed the opposite gender-role behaviours
    to those seen in most countries, as the women were 
    dominant, impersonal and managerial and the men were more
    emotionally dependent.
- Mead originally concluded that these differences highlight
  the impact of culture on gender.

## Androgyny
- This is the psychological state of possessing a balance of
  male and female psychological characteristics.
- It comes from the Greek 'Andro' meaning male, and 'gyny'
  meaning female describing an identity between masculine
  and feminine characteristics.
- For example, a man may possess the emotional skills to be
  caring, but also is competetive and ambitious. This is 
  thought to be advantageous, as the person can respond 
  appropriately to many different situations in life.
- When necessary, they can be empathetic, and when another
  situation demands that they are competetive, they can
  do this as well.
- Androgynous people are argued to be more mentally healthy.

## The BSRI
- Sandra Bem (1974) devised a way of measuring the extent to
  which a person is androgynous. She introduced the idea of
  psychological androgyny and proposed that they are 
  gender-neutral - neither masculine nor feminine with 
  combinations of traits from both sexes.
- Bem argued that the traditional view that rigid sex roles 
  were important for mental heatlth with incorrect and it 
  was actually psychologically more healthy to avoid fixed
  sex-role stereotypes.
- Bem argued that men and women should feel free to adopt to 
  a variety of masculine and feminine type behaviours to 
  suit their personality, i.e. if men enjoyed cooking or 
  being gentle, they shouldn't have to be stifled.

- The aim of the was to examine psychological androgyny and
  provide empirical evidence to show the advantage of 
  shared masculine and feminine personality vs a sex-typed
  categorization.
- The sample was 100 stanford undergraduate students.
- First, Bem compiled a list of 200 personality 
  characteristics that seemed positively valued and 
  stereotypically masculine and feminine, as well as 200 
  gender-neutral characteristics (seemingly neither feminine
  and masculine) which were either negative or positive
  (half and half).
  

# Chromosomes and hormones
- Chromosomes are thread-like structures located inside the
nucleus of animal and plant cells.
- Each chromosome is made up of DNA tightly coiled many times
  around proteins called histones that support its structure.
- DNA contains the specific instructions that make each type
  of living creature unique.
- Males and females differ in a pair of chromosomes known as 
  the sex chromosomes.

- Each person has 23 pair of chromosomes. Each of these pairs 
  carry hundreds of genes containing instructions on physical 
  and behavioural characteristics.
- One pair fo chromosomes (23rd pair) are called the sex 
  chromosomes, because they determine an individual's sex.
- There is a direct link between an individual's chromosomal sex (XX or XY) and their external
  and internal genetalia.
- Biology explains how an individual acquires their sex genetically. It may also explain some 
  aspects of gender becuase people 'feel' masculine or feminine in part because of the 
  genetalia they have.

- Hormones are chemicals released by glands that are responsible for regulating vital bodily functions,
  such as growth, metabolism and mood.
- Testosterone is the main male sex hormone that is involved in the development of male sex organs and
  secondary sexual characteristics, such as increased muscle growth and bone density.
- Estrogen is the primary female sex hormone responsible for the development of female reproductive organs 
  and secondary sexual characteristics.
- Oxytocin is a hormone and neurotransmitter produced by the hypothamulus and released by the pituary gland
  that is involved in childbirth, lactation and social bonding.

## Hormones
- Chromosomes initially determine a person's sex but gender development is governed which are produced both
  prenatally and in puberty.
- Hormones influence the development of genitalia and/or affect the development of the brain, both of which
  have an influence on gender behaviour.

- Testosterone:
  - As a male hormone, which develops the male sex organs during foetal development.
  - Testosterone has an affect on brain development prenatally and also later in childhood.
  - During puberty, there is a surge of testosterone which causes secondary sexual characteristics.
  - Testosterone has behavioural effects and is associated to aggression.
  - Testosterone has an evolutionary explanation with high levels allowign males to compete for 
    opportunities to mate with fertile females as well as taking on the hunter role.

- Oestrogen:
  - A female hormone which develops female sexual characteristics and is linked with emotional changes
    during the menstrual cycle (for example, increased irritability).
  - This develops secondary sexual characteristics from puberty onwards.
  - It controls the menstrual cycle and is also important in pregnancy.
  - It also causes PMT - premenstrual tension or PMS when it becomes a diagnosable disorder.

- Oxytocin:
  - A hormone produced by the pituary gland in both men and women.
  - Oxytocin reduces the stress hormone cortisol and facilitates bonding - this is a reason that it is
    reffered to as the 'love hormone'.
  - It is released in large doses after childbirth, making the mother feel a strong emotional connection
    to their baby as well as lactating.
  - It is produced in lower quantities in men, and this fuels the stereotype that men are less interested
    in intimacy and closeness; however, research shows that it is produced in equal amounts in both sexes
    during amorous activities.

- When the Y chromosome of the foetus is detected by the baby's brain, approximately two months after 
  conception, this slows down the oestrogen production. Testosterone production increases and the baby
  continues to grow as a boy.

# Cognitive explanations

- According to Kohlberg, understanding of gender is similar to
  intellectual development, therefore as a child grows, 
  their understanding of gender becomes more sophisticated.
- Cognitive theories suggest cognition has an impact on
  gender behaviour, emphasising that environmental factors
  and brain development influence congnition influences
  gender behaviour.

## Kohlberg's cognitive-development theory of gender
- Stage 1: Gender identity (2-3.5 years):
  - Children have the ability to correctly identify 
    themselves and others as male or female, although 
    labelling is based more on appearance than reality, e.g.
    child might label a man with long hair 'a girl'.
  - However, at this stage, children do not realise that
    boys grow into men and girls grow into women.
  - Gender may change over time and situations.

- Stage 2: Gender stability (3.5-4.5 years):
  - Recognition that the child's own gender will not change
    over time.
  - However, children may believe that other people's 
    gender can change in different situations. They still rely
    on appearance.

- Stage 3: Gender constancy/consistency (6-7 years):
  - The child recognises that gender is independent of 
    clothing/hair and so on, and gender is constant over time
    and situations.
  - At this point, Kohlberg believed that full gender 
    understanding has developed and children became 
    interested in, and manifest, gender typical behaviour.
  - Children in the stage are no longer egocentric so can
    appreciate that other's thoughts and feelings are
    different to theirs.

- Role models:
  - Once they reach gender constancy, children will begin to
    seek same-sex role models who they will identify with and 
    wish to imitate. They will not do this before this stage,
    as they think that 'gender' is still something that could
    change.

## Evaluation: Strengths
- Kohlberg's theory recognises the role of the child in
  their own gender development therfore there is an element
  of choice and free will.
- Kohlberg's theory is supported by research with children
  and their parents, including cross-cultural research 
  (Munroe et al, (1983); Halim et al, (2013));
- Staby and Frey (1975) asked young children: 'were you a 
  little girl or a little boy when you were a baby?' and 
  'when you grow up will you be a mummy or a daddy?'.
  - Children did not recognise that these traits were stable
    over time until they were 3 or 4 years old, supporting
    the predictions of the theory.
  - In another study, they found children in the gender 
    constancy stage spent longer looking at same-sex adults,
    supporting the idea of role models.

## Evaluations: Limitations
- Kohlberg's theory concentrates on cognitive factors and
  ignores the possible social influences from parents and
  friends, thus disregards the nature of these interactions.
- The theory does not account for individuals who identify
  as nonbinary, transgender or gender fluid.
- There is evidence that children may acquire a fixed sense
  of gender before age 6-7 - Bussey and Bandura (1992) found
  that 4 year olds reported feeling 'awful' about playing
  with gender-innapopriate toys. This weakens the prediction
  of Kohlberg's theory.
- There are methodological issues with interviewing children
  to investigage cognitive theories. Children may not have
  the vocabulary to adequately express what they're thinking.

## Issues and debates
- Kohlberg's theory is culturally biased as it is based on
  western, binary social norms.
- Kohlberg's theory adopts a free will perspective, as 
  children are seen as active thinkers.
- This in contrast with more deterministic views, which see
  gendered behaviour as largerly imposed.

## Gender schema theory
- Martin and Halverson (1983) suggest that children's
  understanding of gender increases with age.
- Assumes that children learn actively rather than through
  observation.

- Gender schema is a generalised representation of what
  we consider to be prototypically male or female.
- Marting and Halverson believed that gender schemas start
  to form from the age of 2 to 3 when they establish a
  gender identity.

- Schemata can include a wide range of information from
  expected behaviours to associated experiences or emotions.
- Early schemata are often based on stereotypes.

- Children develop greater awareness of schematic information
  relevant to their own gender (ingroup) rather than the
  opposite gender (outgroup).
- More complete gender concepts develop from the age of 8
  years.
- Identification with the ingroup raises self-esteem, fosters
  a sense of belonging, aids identification of role models 
  and helps children fir into a social context.

## Marting and Halverson (1983)

- When asked to recall gender-inconsistent behaviour, children

## Psychodynamic and SLT eplanations of gender development
- Freud (1901) believed that children were born gender-
  neutral and their gender identity developed in 
  unconscious psychosexual stages.
- Freud's five psychosexual stages:
  - Oral, anal, phalic, latent, genital.
- The first two stages are gender-neutral stages - the oral
  and anal stages, when there is no visible difference 
  between the behaviour of boys and girls.
  - Therefore, children under the age of 3 have no gender
    identity and no notion of the gender concept.

## Pre-phalic children
- Gender development occurs during the phalic stage (between
  ages 3 and 6).
  - The phalic stage is when the focus of the libido moves
    to the genitals.
- Oedipus and Electra complex play an important role in
  the development of the child's gender identity.

## Oedipus complex
- During the phalic stage, boys develop a sexual desire
  for their mother.
- Father perceived as rival and is hated for keeping the boy
  away from his mother.
- Father is also a powerful figure the boy cannot defeat,
  - Leads to castration anxiety for fear of them discovering
    the boys desire for their mother.
- To resolve the complex, boys must give up the desire for 
  their mother and identify with the father thus taking on
  the male gender role.

## Electra complex
- During the phalic stage, girls develop penis envy.
- Develop desire for their father and feel they are competing
  with their mother.
- Girls both see thier mother as a rival and blame her for
  their 'castration'.
- To resolve this complex girls need to replace their wish
  for a penis with the wish for a baby which will allow them
  to identify with their mother and take on the female gender
  role.

## Identification and internalisation
- Both boys and girls must learn to identify with their 
  same sex parent to resolve their complex.
- This involves taking on the attitudes and values of the 
  father/mother.
- Taking on gender identity through internalisation of the
  gendered attitudes and behaviours.
- Gender identity cannot fully develop in a child until the 
  Oedipus/Electra complex is resolved.

- If the young child does not successfully resolve the
  crisis and rorm their own identity, then psychological
  disturbance could be the result, with girls being
  'father-fixated' and boys being 'mother-fixed', instead of
  identifying with the same-sex parent.
- Freud believed that a girl's identification with her mother
  was more passive and submissive than a boy's identification
  with his father, which was active and dominant.
- Frued saw women as inferior to men, whose gender development
  was the norm, with the female gender development being
  a deviant version.
